1. Behavioral Typologies (Cluster Analysis)
Meaning Behind the Data: Cluster analysis of student behaviors reveals that students are not a monolith. They fall into four distinct archetypes. While 32% are "Strategic Optimizers" who balance speed and quality, nearly 28% are "Dialogic Learners" who engage in deep, conversation-style learning. This suggests support strategies must be tailored: Optimizers need templates, while Dialogic Learners need help with synthesis and closure.

2. The "SAGE" Verification Core
Meaning Behind the Data: The study used the SAGE framework to force a "Verification" step. The results are striking: 73% of students complied with the requirement to verify, debunking the idea that students will always take the easy route. However, the "Application Bottleneck" is visible in the center chart: Students struggled most (red bar) when asked to provide specific recommendations, proving that AI is good at summarizing but poor at applying context—this is where the human student adds value.

3. Learning Trajectory
Meaning Behind the Data: How do students learn to prompt? The data suggests a "Goldilocks Zone" of 4-8 prompts where students feel most productive. Those who used 15+ prompts often reported "confusion spirals," where more AI interaction led to worse outputs. Most importantly, the "Pivot" chart (right) shows 95% of students want to change their strategy next time, indicating high metacognitive awareness and a desire to move from "one-shot" attempts to iterative dialogue.

Prompt Frequency: The "Goldilocks Zone"

4. Depth of Engagement: Myth vs. Reality
Meaning Behind the Data: A common fear is that AI leads to passivity. The data refutes this. 81% of students (Green) engaged in "Deep" behaviors—either completely rewriting the output or asking follow-up questions to refine it. Only a minority (14%) engaged in "Surface" behaviors like minor formatting. This proves that for most students, AI functions as a drafting partner that stimulates revision, rather than a ghostwriter that replaces it.

5. Drivers Beyond Time: Equity & Inclusion
Meaning Behind the Data: Why do students use AI? While "Time Pressure" is the obvious #1, the #2 driver is "English Confidence" (46%). This suggests that for international and NESB students, AI is not just a shortcut, but an equity tool—a "levelling mechanism" that allows them to express their ideas without the barrier of language proficiency.

7. Thematic Analysis of Concerns & Needs
Meaning Behind the Data: Analyzing the open-ended reflections reveals a hierarchy of needs. 45% of all concerns relate to "Policy Clarity"—students are anxious about vague rules and fear accidental plagiarism. When it comes to training (right chart), they don't want generic advice; they want specific "Prompt Engineering" (35%) and tool overviews. They are asking for a curriculum that is practical and hands-on.

8. Actionable Roadmap (Recommendations)
Meaning Behind the Data: Based on the "Support Wishlist" and behavioral gaps, this roadmap outlines a phased institutional response. It moves from the immediate need for clarity (stopping the anxiety), to the mid-term need for competency (building skills), to the long-term need for integration (embedding AI into the fabric of the degree).
Immediate Term
Clarify the "Rules of Engagement"
  • Define "Acceptable Use" with concrete examples.
  • Publish a "Crystal Clear" unified policy.
  • Differentiate between "Idea Generation" vs "Drafting".
Mid Term
Pedagogy of Exemplars
  • Show "Side-by-Side" examples (Good vs. Bad AI).
  • Run "Prompt Engineering" workshops (35% demand).
  • Create safe "Sandbox" environments for failure.
Long Term
Infrastructure & Integration
  • Launch Dedicated AI Support Hub (40% demand).
  • Move to Process-Based Assessment.
  • Integrate AI literacy into core curriculum.
